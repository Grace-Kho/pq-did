II. R ELATED W ORK
We review decentralised identity, post-quantum selfsovereign identity (SSI), anonymous credentials and revocation, focusing on their relationship to PQ-DID’s credential
lifecycle and privacy model.
A. Decentralised Identity and Privacy-Preserving Credentials
The W3C DID and Verifiable Credentials specifications
define identifier and credential data models; security depends
on the selected cryptographic mechanisms [13], [14]. AnonCreds v1.0 combines Camenisch–Lysyanskaya signatures over
RSA groups with pairing-based revocation, while the BBS
cryptosuite uses pairing-based signatures. Both support selective disclosure and unlinkable presentations under classical
assumptions [15], [16].
DISC supports multi-authority credential aggregation, unlinkable authentication and batch verification [17]. DP-DID
addresses dynamic and proactive identity management [18],
while FRAC expresses access-control rules and addresses credential theft and revoked-credential reuse [19]. PQ-DID studies quantum-resistant credential presentation and revocationrelated privacy. Aggregation, proactive key protection and a
general-purpose access-control language are outside its scope.
B. Post-Quantum Decentralised Identity and SSI
Pino et al. studied hybrid post-quantum and classical credentials and presentations for IoT [20]. Solavagione and Vesco
implemented pure post-quantum and hybrid SSI in IOTA
Identity, covering DID, VC and VP workflows [21]. Zeydan et
al. developed post-quantum blockchain-based identity management for resource sharing in open radio access networks [22].
Presentation privacy has also been addressed directly. Margaria et al. adapted Bootle et al.’s lattice anonymous-credential
framework to SSI and implemented selective disclosure in

JOURNAL OF LATEX CLASS FILES, VOL. 14, NO. 8, AUGUST 2021

C++ [23], [24]. Boi et al. investigated ZK-STARKs for postquantum SSI and attribute-based authentication on edge devices [25]. The W3C Quantum-Resistant Cryptosuites draft
specifies Data Integrity mechanisms based on post-quantum
signatures [26]. These works cover both signature migration
and privacy-preserving presentation, providing direct comparators for PQ-DID.
C. Post-Quantum Anonymous Credentials and Attestation
Bootle et al.’s BLNS framework supports selective disclosure and unlinkable presentations under specialised lattice assumptions, with privacy against coalitions including malicious
issuers, holders and verifiers [24]. Argo et al. instantiated
anonymous credentials with explicit proof encodings and a
C implementation [27]. Both works also discuss revocation
extensions and provide comparators for PQ-DAA’s credentialproof layer.
Chen et al. constructed lattice-based direct anonymous attestation using collaborative, segregated non-interactive zero
knowledge [28]. A trusted platform module and its host jointly
generate attestations, with basename-controlled linkability and
key-based revocation. Their hardware trust and collaborative proving architecture differ from PQ-DID’s issuer–holder–
verifier model.
D. Revocation and Lifecycle Privacy
Margaria et al. use validity epochs and issuer-provided
credential updates [23]. PQ-DID proves that a hidden, issuercertified identifier corresponds to an unrevoked leaf in a
Merkle revocation tree; public updates support local witness
maintenance. The mechanisms differ in status representation
and update procedures, whose costs require empirical comparison. The IRAC preprint combines issuer hiding, flexible
credentials and decentralised revocation with a classical implementation [29]; PQ-DID retains a public issuer.
PQ-DID binds selective disclosure and non-revocation to the
same credential and presentation context. Its post-revocation
unlinkability model includes historical presentations and subsequent public revocation information. The baseline permits
colluding verifiers but assumes honest issuers and revocation
managers whose private records remain undisclosed. Challenge holders remain uncorrupted throughout; issuer, schema,
policy, epoch and disclosed attributes are public. These restrictions distinguish its guarantees from malicious-issuer privacy
and security after holder compromise.

A. Architecture and Object Mapping
DID Core specifies identifiers, documents and method operations; the Verifiable Credentials (VC) data model specifies

3

credentials and presentations [13], [14]. PQ-DID follows the
issuer–holder–verifier workflow: publication and resolution
manage DID records, issuance produces a private credential record vc, presentation produces disclosed claims and a
PQ-DAA proof vp, and revocation updates credential status
through rse . This mapping is architectural: the protocol encodings alone do not establish VC/VP data-model conformance
or standardisation of the reference DID method. Conforming
representations require the corresponding data-model properties and proof/status extensions.
The reference method retains controller public keys in
signed method records outside the minimal DID document.
Credential authentication uses a separate holder secret xH .
Although a holder DID may be publicly registered, presentations neither disclose nor resolve it unless the holder-approved
policy requires disclosure.
B. Roles and Trust Assumptions
The issuer validates evidence and approves the exact attribute vector, including the issuance-time DID and version
reference (did, vD ). The holder proves knowledge of its
binding secret. The issuer checks controller authorisation and
certifies the approved vector, holder binding and allocated revocation identifier. Issuance uses an authenticated confidential
channel; the holder retains its secret, credential and revocation
witness locally. Certification establishes issuer approval, the
validity of external evidence depends on the issuer.
Application configuration fixes accepted issuer/key/schema
instances, revocation namespaces and trusted authority, registry and audience-request keys. Resolution validates method
state and controller authorisation, issuer trust remains an application decision. The revocation manager processes authorised
requests and publishes authenticated states and updates, from
which holders update witnesses locally.
The model assumes correctly generated parameters, consistent registry operations and an authenticated current-state
service. The verifier’s final ordered state read fixes the verification epoch, unavailable or inconsistent state causes rejection.
Authenticity of an old signed state does not establish freshness.
Holders approve authenticated requests, verifiers enforce the
expected audience, session, policy and expiry, with trusted
time and atomic challenge consumption. These service assumptions remain explicit in the lifecycle proofs.
C. Adversaries and Public Information
Adversaries are quantum polynomial-time (QPT), with classical protocol queries and quantum access to the proof random oracle. Enrolments are sequential, presentations may
be repeated across colluding verifiers under changing supported policies. Corrupted holders may collude with verifiers.
Setup, the targeted issuer, revocation manager and currentstate service remain honest, and DID-method integrity is
assumed. Privacy protects holders uncorrupted throughout the
experiment, including after revocation, and excludes authority–
verifier collusion and disclosure of private authority records.
Acceptance attacks target an honest verifier.

D. Cryptographic Interfaces
Signatures. KeyGen, Sign and Verify require correctness
and post-quantum EUF-CMA security with classical signing
queries. Section VII uses ML-DSA-65 with separate signing contexts for credentials and lifecycle messages [30], its
bounded-operation losses are accounted for in Section VIII.
Holder binding and certification. BindRep recovers
the certified representation B, BindOpen checks its secret/message opening, and CredValid additionally checks issuer certification and identifier consistency. The selected B
contains a SHA3-384 holder-secret digest and the canonical
approved attribute vector. Binding requires quantum collision

resistance; secrecy of the uniformly sampled secret given
its digest additionally requires quantum preimage resistance.
Attributes are visible to the issuer, presentation privacy is
supplied by the proof.
Revocation. The status mechanism supplies state updates,
witness generation, NRVerify and UpdateWit, requiring correctness and adaptive non-membership soundness. The selected mechanism is a SHA3-384 Merkle tree of revoked bits:
an unrevoked credential has a path authenticating a zero leaf.
State authentication and freshness are enforced by the lifecycle
interfaces.
Arguments of knowledge. Prove and Check operate on
the complete relation Rauth of Section V-B, jointly checking
valid encodings, holder binding, issuer certification, authorised
disclosure and non-revocation for the same certified identifier, with the presentation context bound to the statement.
The selected Boolean MPC-in-the-head protocol uses raw
random tapes, salted view commitments and Fiat–Shamir
challenges [31]. Its requirements are completeness, adaptive
quantum zero knowledge and history-preserving completewitness extraction. Sections VII and VIII specify the circuits
and derive the resource-dependent QROM bounds [32], [33].
The baseline supports disclosure and public checks on disclosed values, hidden-attribute predicates require an additional
relation.

A. Protocol State and Presentation Context
We adopt the roles and trust assumptions of Section III.
Each instance fixes one issuer verification-key version, one

Current-state assumption. The authenticated service

D. DID Binding and Security Objectives
DID binding gives a credential issuance-time controllerauthorisation semantics. The issuer validates control of
(did, vD ) in the same session in which that reference and
the approved attributes are bound to the holder secret. Later
presentations prove consistency with that certified statement
while concealing the DID. This links issuance to the selected
method’s identifier-management rules [13]; it does not establish current control of a hidden DID after a controller change.
Such changes require an application policy for revocation
or reissuance. Current hidden-DID control would require an
additional authenticated current-state relation.
Section VI formalises authorised certification, preservation
of the certified holder binding, knowledge-based authentication soundness, adaptive presentation privacy, revocation
soundness and post-revocation unlinkability. Session binding and one-time challenge consumption supply replay resistance at the lifecycle layer. Privacy is relative to authorised public disclosures and the permitted corruption model.
Post-revocation unlinkability protects historical presentations
against later public revocation information while holder secrets
remain uncompromised; it is distinct from forward security
after secret-key exposure.
